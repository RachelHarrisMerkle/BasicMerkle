## UX Theory cards

### Aesthetic-Usability Effect

People tend to judge a design that looks good as easier to use, whether or not it is.

First studied in human-computer interaction in 1995 by Masaaki Kurosu and Kaori Kashimura of the Hitachi Design Center. They showed 26 variations of an ATM interface to 252 participants and found that ratings of beauty tracked ratings of apparent ease of use more closely than they tracked actual ease of use.

Cite as: Masaaki Kurosu and Kaori Kashimura, Hitachi Design Center (1995).

### Doherty Threshold

People stay engaged and productive when the system responds within about 400 milliseconds, so that neither side is waiting on the other.

In 1982 Walter J. Doherty and Ahrvind J. Thadani of IBM published research arguing that computer response time should be about 400 ms, not the 2 seconds that had been the accepted standard, because productivity rose sharply as response time fell.

Cite as: Walter J. Doherty and Ahrvind J. Thadani, IBM (1982).

### Fitts' Law

The time to hit a target depends on how far away it is and how big it is. Small, distant targets are slow and error-prone.

Psychologist Paul Fitts showed in 1954 that the time to move to a target increases with the distance to it and decreases with its width, and that fast movements to small targets produce more errors. The law has been applied to pointing devices since the early days of human-computer interaction research.

Cite as: Paul Fitts, "The information capacity of the human motor system in controlling the amplitude of movement", Journal of Experimental Psychology (1954).

### Goal-Gradient Effect

People speed up and try harder the closer they get to a goal.

Behaviorist Clark Hull proposed the goal-gradient hypothesis in 1932 and reported in 1934 that rats ran faster as they neared food at the end of a runway. Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng (2006) showed the pattern in human reward programs. In a related car-wash study by Nunes and Drèze (2006), 34% of customers given a 10-stamp card with 2 stamps already filled completed it, compared with 19% given an empty 8-stamp card needing the same number of purchases.

Cite as: Clark Hull (1932); Ran Kivetz, Oleg Urminsky and Yuhuang Zheng (2006).

### Hick's Law

The more options there are, and the more complex they are, the longer a decision takes.

Named for British psychologist William Edmund Hick and American psychologist Ray Hyman, whose experiments in 1952 and 1953 measured how reaction time rises with the number of possible stimuli. The relationship they found is logarithmic.

Cite as: William Edmund Hick (1952) and Ray Hyman (1953).

### Jakob's Law

People spend most of their time on other products, so they expect yours to work the way those do.

Stated by usability researcher Jakob Nielsen in 2000 as the "law of the internet user experience". Nielsen co-founded the Nielsen Norman Group with Don Norman and is known for discount usability methods and heuristic evaluation.

Cite as: Jakob Nielsen, Nielsen Norman Group (2000).

### Law of Common Region

Elements inside the same clearly bounded area are seen as a group.

The classic Gestalt grouping principles (proximity, similarity, continuity, closure) were set out by Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Köhler in the early 20th century. Common region was added to the list by Stephen Palmer in 1992.

Cite as: Stephen Palmer, "Common region: A new principle of perceptual grouping", Cognitive Psychology (1992).

### Law of Proximity

Things that are close together are seen as belonging together.

One of the original Gestalt principles of perceptual grouping, described by Max Wertheimer in 1923 and developed with Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Köhler in the early 20th century.

Cite as: Max Wertheimer, "Laws of organization in perceptual forms" (1923).

### Law of Prägnanz

People read a complex or ambiguous image as the simplest form they can, because that takes the least mental effort.

Prägnanz, often translated as "good figure" or "simplicity", is the central principle of Gestalt psychology as developed by Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Köhler in the early 20th century: perception tends toward the simplest, most stable organization available.

Cite as: Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Köhler, Gestalt psychology (early 20th century).

### Law of Similarity

Things that look alike are seen as related, even when they are apart.

One of the original Gestalt grouping principles described by Max Wertheimer in 1923 and developed with Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Köhler.

Cite as: Max Wertheimer, "Laws of organization in perceptual forms" (1923).

### Law of Uniform Connectedness

Elements that are visually connected are seen as more related than elements that are not.

Proposed by Stephen Palmer and Irvin Rock in 1994 as an addition to the classic Gestalt grouping principles of Wertheimer, Koffka and Köhler. They argued that connected regions of uniform visual properties are perceived as single units before other grouping takes place.

Cite as: Stephen Palmer and Irvin Rock, "Rethinking perceptual organization: The role of uniform connectedness", Psychonomic Bulletin and Review (1994).

### Miller's Law

An average person can hold about seven items, give or take two, in working memory.

George A. Miller's 1956 paper reported that the span of immediate memory, and of absolute judgment, was limited to about seven items. Miller himself treated the number with some humor, and the paper's lasting point is that recoding information into chunks extends what people can hold. Nelson Cowan's 2001 review argued the limit is closer to four chunks.

Cite as: George A. Miller, "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two", Psychological Review (1956).

### Occam's Razor

Of the explanations or solutions that work equally well, prefer the one that assumes the least.

A principle of parsimony attributed to William of Ockham, an English Franciscan friar and philosopher of the 14th century. It is a rule of thumb for choosing between hypotheses, borrowed by design as an argument for removing what is not needed.

Cite as: Attributed to William of Ockham (c. 1287 to 1347).

### Pareto Principle

In many situations a small share of the causes produces a large share of the effects, often summarized as 80% from 20%.

Named for economist Vilfredo Pareto, who observed around 1900 that roughly 80% of the land in Italy was owned by 20% of the population. Quality-management pioneer Joseph Juran later generalized the pattern and gave it Pareto's name.

Cite as: Named for Vilfredo Pareto; generalized by Joseph Juran.

### Parkinson's Law

Work expands to fill the time available for it.

Stated by naval historian Cyril Northcote Parkinson in the opening line of a satirical essay in The Economist in 1955, drawn from his experience of the British Civil Service, and later published in the book Parkinson's Law.

Cite as: Cyril Northcote Parkinson, The Economist (1955).

### Postel's Law

Be liberal in what you accept and conservative in what you send.

Internet pioneer Jon Postel wrote the principle into the 1980 specification of TCP: implementations should be conservative in what they do and liberal in what they accept from others. It is also known as the robustness principle, and it carries over to interfaces as tolerance for human input.

Cite as: Jon Postel, the TCP specification (RFC 761, 1980).

### Peak-End Rule

People judge an experience mostly by how it felt at its most intense moment and at its end, not by the average of every moment.

A 1993 study by Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier had participants hold a hand in 14 degree Celsius water for 60 seconds, and in another trial for the same 60 seconds plus 30 more while the water warmed to 15 degrees. Most chose to repeat the longer trial: it contained more discomfort in total, but it ended less badly.

Cite as: Daniel Kahneman, Barbara Fredrickson, Charles Schreiber and Donald Redelmeier, "When More Pain Is Preferred to Less: Adding a Better End", Psychological Science (1993).

### Serial Position Effect

People remember the first and last items in a series best, and the middle ones least.

The term is credited to Hermann Ebbinghaus, whose memory experiments in the 1880s showed that recall depends on an item's position in a sequence. Bennet Murdock's 1962 free-recall studies mapped the familiar U-shaped curve of primacy and recency.

Cite as: Hermann Ebbinghaus (1885); Bennet Murdock (1962).

### Tesler's Law

Every process has some complexity that cannot be removed. It can only be moved between the system and the person using it.

Larry Tesler, a pioneer of interaction design who worked at Xerox PARC and Apple, formulated the law of conservation of complexity in the mid-1980s. He argued that an engineer should spend extra time reducing an application's complexity instead of making millions of users each spend extra time on it. Bruce Tognazzini has added that people tend to use up simplification by attempting harder tasks.

Cite as: Larry Tesler, the law of conservation of complexity (mid-1980s).

### Von Restorff Effect

When several similar things are shown together, the one that differs is the one most likely to be remembered.

German psychiatrist and pediatrician Hedwig von Restorff reported in a 1933 study that when people were shown a list of similar items with one item that differed, they remembered the different item better. The finding is also called the isolation effect.

Cite as: Hedwig von Restorff (1933).

### Zeigarnik Effect

People remember unfinished or interrupted tasks better than finished ones.

Soviet psychologist Bluma Zeigarnik, working in Berlin with Kurt Lewin, published a study in 1927 in which participants recalled interrupted tasks better than ones they had completed. The effect has had mixed results in later replications, so use it as a design prompt, not a guarantee.

Cite as: Bluma Zeigarnik (1927).

### Campbell's Law

The more a number is used to make decisions, the more people will bend their behavior to move the number, and the less the number tells you.

Formulated by psychologist and social scientist Donald T. Campbell in the 1970s: the more a quantitative social indicator is used for decision-making, the more it is subject to corruption pressures and the more it distorts the processes it was meant to monitor. It is closely related to Goodhart's law in economics.

Cite as: Donald T. Campbell, "Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change" (1976).

### Stroop Effect

When two attributes of the same thing conflict, people are slower and make more mistakes.

Named for John Ridley Stroop, whose 1935 paper reported that naming the ink color of a color word is slower when the word names a different color. Similar interference had been described in Germany by Erich Jaensch in 1929.

Cite as: John Ridley Stroop, "Studies of interference in serial verbal reactions", Journal of Experimental Psychology (1935).

### Simon Effect

People respond faster and more accurately when a signal appears in the same relative location as the response it calls for.

From a 1967 study by J. Richard Simon and Alan Rudell. Participants heard the words "left" or "right" in one ear and pressed the matching key. Responses were slower when the word arrived in the ear opposite to the correct key, although ear position had nothing to do with the task.

Cite as: J. Richard Simon and Alan Rudell (1967).

### Accot-Zhai Steering Law

The time it takes to steer a pointer or finger through a path depends on how long and how narrow the path is. Long, narrow paths are slow and easy to fall out of.

Johnny Accot and Shumin Zhai derived the steering law from Fitts' law in 1997 and verified it experimentally for human-computer interaction. Similar laws had been found independently by Rashevsky (1959) and Drury (1971). For a straight tunnel, steering time grows with its length divided by its width.

Cite as: Johnny Accot and Shumin Zhai (1997).

### Law of Closure

People fill in missing parts of a shape to see a complete whole.

One of the Gestalt principles of perception developed by Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Köhler in the early 20th century: people tend to perceive incomplete figures as complete.

Cite as: Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Köhler, Gestalt psychology (early 20th century).

### Law of Continuity

Elements arranged along a line or curve are seen as related and are followed by the eye in that direction.

One of the original Gestalt grouping principles, described by Max Wertheimer in 1923 as "good continuation": elements that follow a smooth line or curve are perceived as belonging together.

Cite as: Max Wertheimer, "Laws of organization in perceptual forms" (1923).

### Paradox of the Active User

People do not read instructions. They start using the product straight away.

Described by John M. Carroll and Mary Beth Rosson in a 1987 chapter of Interfacing Thought: Cognitive Aspects of Human-Computer Interaction. Studying new users of office software, they found people skipped the manuals and started working at once, even when that led to errors and dead ends.

Cite as: John M. Carroll and Mary Beth Rosson, in Interfacing Thought (1987).

### The Principle of Least Effort

People take the path that costs them the least mental and physical effort to get the job done.

Proposed by Harvard linguist George Kingsley Zipf in his 1949 book Human Behavior and the Principle of Least Effort, which grew out of his statistical study of word frequency. It has since been applied widely, including to how people seek information.

Cite as: George Kingsley Zipf, Human Behavior and the Principle of Least Effort (1949).

## Psychology Concept cards

### Cognitive Load

Cognitive load is the mental effort a person has to spend to understand and use an interface.

Cognitive load theory was developed by educational psychologist John Sweller in the late 1980s, building on George Miller's work on the limits of information processing. Sweller's 1988 paper in Cognitive Science is the usual starting reference.

Cite as: John Sweller, "Cognitive load during problem solving: Effects on learning", Cognitive Science (1988).

### Cognitive Bias

Cognitive biases are predictable errors in judgment that come from the mental shortcuts people use to decide quickly.

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman introduced the modern study of cognitive biases in the early 1970s, showing through repeatable experiments that human judgment departs from the predictions of rational choice theory in systematic ways.

Cite as: Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, work on heuristics and biases from the early 1970s.

### Cognitive Dissonance

In the deck's interface sense: the discomfort people feel when a control or screen that looks intuitive delivers a result they did not expect.

The term comes from social psychologist Leon Festinger, whose 1957 book A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance proposed that people are uncomfortable holding inconsistent beliefs and are motivated to reduce the inconsistency. The deck applies the idea to the gap between what an interface appears to promise and what it does.

Cite as: Leon Festinger, A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance (1957).

### Mental Model

A mental model is what a person believes about how something works, built from their past experience.

The idea is usually traced to Kenneth Craik's 1943 book The Nature of Explanation, which proposed that people carry small-scale models of the world in their heads and use them to predict events. It has been central to human-computer interaction since the 1980s.

Cite as: Kenneth Craik, The Nature of Explanation (1943).

### Chunking

Chunking is breaking information into pieces and grouping the pieces into meaningful units, so people handle a few groups instead of many items.

The term comes from George A. Miller's 1956 paper on the limits of short-term memory, which observed that people can hold more information when they recode it into larger meaningful units.

Cite as: George A. Miller, "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two", Psychological Review (1956).

### Selective Attention

People attend to a small part of what is in front of them, usually the part related to their current goal, and filter out the rest.

The modern study of selective attention began with Colin Cherry's 1953 work on how people follow one voice among many, and Donald Broadbent's 1958 filter model, which proposed that limited processing capacity forces early selection of what to attend to. The term "banner blindness" comes from Benway and Lane (1998).

Cite as: Colin Cherry (1953) and Donald Broadbent's filter model (1958).

### Analysis Paralysis

Analysis paralysis is failing to decide because the decision has been over-thought, often because there are too many options or they are too hard to compare.

The phrase "paralysis by analysis" appears in management writing by the 1950s and the idea is far older. The best-known experiment is Iyengar and Lepper's jam study (2000): shoppers offered 24 jams were more likely to stop and look than shoppers offered 6, but far less likely to buy (about 3% compared with about 30%). A 2010 meta-analysis by Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd found the average effect across studies close to zero, so treat choice overload as real but conditional.

Cite as: Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper, "When Choice Is Demotivating", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology (2000).

### Flow

Flow is the state of being fully absorbed in an activity, with focused attention, a sense of control and enjoyment of the activity itself.

Psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi named and described flow in the 1970s, most fully in Beyond Boredom and Anxiety (1975), from studies of people absorbed in activities such as climbing, chess and music.

Cite as: Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, Beyond Boredom and Anxiety (1975).

### Short-Term Memory

Short-term memory holds a small amount of information for a short time. Interfaces should not depend on it.

The split between short-term and long-term memory dates from the 19th century. The best-known account is the modal model published by Richard Atkinson and Richard Shiffrin in 1968. George Miller's 1956 paper gave the "seven, plus or minus two" capacity estimate; Nelson Cowan's 2001 review argued for about four chunks.

Cite as: Richard Atkinson and Richard Shiffrin, the modal model of memory (1968).

## Interaction Principle cards

### Discoverability

People can work out what a product does, how it works and what they can do next by looking at it.

One of Don Norman's fundamental principles of interaction, set out in The Design of Everyday Things (1988, revised 2013). Norman describes discoverability as the result of applying affordances, signifiers, constraints, mapping and feedback well, with the conceptual model tying them together.

Cite as: Don Norman, The Design of Everyday Things (1988, revised 2013).

### Affordances

An affordance is an action that an object makes possible for a particular person. In an interface, the form of a control should suggest what you can do with it.

The word comes from perceptual psychologist James J. Gibson. Don Norman brought it into design in The Design of Everyday Things (1988) and later separated it from the signifier, the perceivable cue that points to the action.

Cite as: James J. Gibson coined the term (1966, 1979); Don Norman applied it to design in The Design of Everyday Things (1988).

### Signifiers

A signifier is any cue you can see or hear that tells you where an action happens and how to do it.

Don Norman introduced the term to separate the cue from the action it points to, most fully in the 2013 revised edition of The Design of Everyday Things. It is one of the concepts he lists as producing discoverability.

Cite as: Don Norman, The Design of Everyday Things (revised edition, 2013).

### Feedback

After every action, the system shows that the action was received and what happened as a result.

One of the fundamental principles of interaction described by Don Norman in The Design of Everyday Things. Norman treats immediate, informative feedback as a requirement for people to understand the result of their actions.

Cite as: Don Norman, The Design of Everyday Things (1988, revised 2013).

### Mapping

Mapping is the relationship between a control and the thing it changes. Good mapping makes that relationship obvious from layout and direction.

Norman borrowed the term from mathematics, where it names the relationship between the elements of two sets. In The Design of Everyday Things he uses stove burners and their knobs as the classic example of good and bad mapping.

Cite as: Don Norman, The Design of Everyday Things (1988, revised 2013).

### Constraints

Constraints limit what people can do so that the right action is the easy one and the wrong action is hard or impossible.

One of Don Norman's fundamental principles of interaction. The four categories (physical, cultural, semantic and logical) come from The Design of Everyday Things.

Cite as: Don Norman, The Design of Everyday Things (1988, revised 2013).

### Conceptual Model

A conceptual model is the simplified explanation of how something works that a person builds from using it, being shown it and being told about it.

Don Norman treats the conceptual model as the principle that ties the others together in The Design of Everyday Things: affordances, signifiers, constraints, mapping and feedback are how a product communicates its model.

Cite as: Don Norman, The Design of Everyday Things (1988, revised 2013).

## UX Method cards

### Card Sorting

A research method where participants sort topics into groups and name the groups, so the structure of a product matches how its users think.

Background: A long-established information architecture research method.

### Design Principles

A short, agreed set of guidelines that frames how a team approaches and settles design problems.

Background: A common design team practice.

### Journey Mapping

A picture of the steps a person goes through to reach a goal, with what they do, think and feel at each one.

Background: A widely used service design and UX method.

### User Personas

A fictional but research-based profile whose characteristics and goals stand for a larger group of real users.

Background: Popularized by Alan Cooper, The Inmates Are Running the Asylum (1999).

### Usability Test

Watching representative users attempt realistic tasks with a design, to find problems and learn how people behave.

Background: A core usability engineering method; see Jakob Nielsen, Usability Engineering (1993).

### User Interview

A one-to-one conversation in which a researcher asks a user about a topic to learn about their behavior and attitudes.

Background: A foundational qualitative research method.

### Affinity Mapping

Sorting qualitative notes into groups on an affinity diagram to find themes and insights.

Background: Based on the KJ method of Jiro Kawakita (1960s).

### UX Survey

A set of questions sent to many users to collect data about their experience with a product.

Background: A standard research method; see the System Usability Scale, John Brooke (1986).

### Contextual Inquiry

A field study that combines observing a small number of users at work with interviewing them, to understand their real practices and behavior.

Background: Hugh Beyer and Karen Holtzblatt, Contextual Design (1998).
