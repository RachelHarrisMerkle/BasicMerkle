# Playbook: onboarding and first run

Sign-up to first value: setup steps, welcome screens, product tours, checklists and the
first empty workspace.

## Laws that matter most

1. **Paradox of the Active User.** New users do not read. They start. Teaching has to
   happen inside the product, at the moment it is needed.
2. **Principle of Least Effort.** Every step before the first useful result is a place to
   leave.
3. **Goal-Gradient Effect and Zeigarnik Effect.** Visible progress and a clear "not
   finished yet" pull people through setup.
4. **Hick's Law.** A new user faced with every feature at once chooses none.
5. **Jakob's Law and Mental Model.** New users map your product onto ones they know. Meet
   that map before you teach anything new.
6. **Peak-End Rule.** The first success is the moment people remember. Make sure it
   happens and is marked.

## Build rules

1. **Define the first win.** Name the one outcome that shows the product working for this
   user (first invoice sent, first board created). Everything in onboarding either leads
   to it or waits until after it.
2. **Get there in as few steps as possible.** Ask only for what the first win needs.
   Move profile details, preferences and team invitations to after it.
3. **Let people in before the wall.** Where the product allows, show value before asking
   for an account, a card or permissions. Ask for a permission at the moment its feature
   is used, with a one-line reason.
4. **No blocking tour.** Do not open with a slide carousel or a multi-step overlay. If a
   tour exists, it is optional, skippable and can be reopened.
5. **Start with something, not nothing.** Open on sample content, a template or a single
   highlighted first action. An empty workspace with no cue is a dead end.
6. **Teach in place.** One short hint beside the control the first time it matters. Show
   with an example or a highlighted element instead of a paragraph.
7. **Show the path.** For setup with more than two steps, show named steps and where the
   person is ("Step 2 of 4: Connect your bank"). Credit steps already done, such as
   creating the account. Only real steps.
8. **Reveal features as they become useful.** Start with the core and introduce the rest
   when the person reaches the point where each helps.
9. **Use the patterns people know** for sign-up, log-in, verification and navigation.
   Onboarding is the wrong place for a new interaction.
10. **Save everything.** If the person leaves midway, return them to where they stopped
    with their input intact, and show what is left.
11. **Mark the first win.** Confirm it plainly, say what it unlocked and offer the next
    step. Keep it brief.
12. **Make setup tasks dismissible.** A checklist for optional tasks can be closed and
    found again later.

## Checks

| Check | Law |
|---|---|
| Count the screens and inputs between landing and the first win. Can any move to later? | Principle of Least Effort |
| No modal tour or carousel blocks first use; any tour can be skipped and reopened. | Paradox of the Active User |
| The first screen after sign-up has one obvious first action or sample content. | Discoverability, Hick's Law |
| Instructions are next to the control they explain, not on a screen before it. | Paradox of the Active User |
| Multi-step setup shows the steps, the current step and how many remain. | Goal-Gradient Effect |
| Progress shown is true: credited steps were completed. | Cognitive Bias |
| Leaving and returning resumes at the same step with input kept. | Zeigarnik Effect |
| Unfinished setup is visible after onboarding and can be dismissed. | Zeigarnik Effect |
| Permissions and payment are requested at the point of need, with a reason. | Principle of Least Effort |
| Sign-up, verification and navigation follow platform conventions. | Jakob's Law |
| The first success is confirmed with a clear next step. | Peak-End Rule |
| Not every feature is introduced at once. | Hick's Law, Cognitive Load |

## Mistakes this screen invites

The welcome tour, the wall of fields, the stepless wizard, the dead end (an empty
workspace), the memory test (instructions on one screen, the task on the next), the
mystery icons.

## When laws conflict here

- **Teach against let them start.** Let them start. Teach one thing at a time, in place
  (Paradox of the Active User over an upfront explanation of everything).
- **Fewer steps against smaller decisions.** Several easy steps beat one dense screen,
  as long as the total stays short and progress is shown.
- **Motivation against honesty.** Progress bars and checklists must reflect real work
  done and real work left.
- **Collect data against first win.** The first win comes first. Ask for the rest later.

## Research that settles what the laws cannot

What the first win should be, and where people stall: user interviews to learn what new
users are trying to do, and a usability test of the first five minutes with people who
have never seen the product.
