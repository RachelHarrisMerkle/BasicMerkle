
# Merkle Copywriting Skill

This skill covers **website copy, digital product copy (UI labels, microcopy, error messages, CTAs), and presentation slides**. For visual design or slide structure, pair with the relevant design skill.

## 1. Who Merkle is — know this before writing a word

Merkle is a **data-driven, outcomes-obsessed, end-to-end transformation consultancy**. That's the full positioning statement; use it sparingly and precisely, not as filler.

The one-line brand promise: **"We power the experience economy."**

Three differentiating pillars — always write with these in mind:

| Pillar | What it means in copy |
|---|---|
| **Data-driven** | Ground claims in numbers. Merkle invented people-based marketing and Merkury. More than 25% of staff are in data science, analytics, or research. |
| **Outcomes-obsessed** | Lead with results, not activities. 33%+ of contracts include performance incentives — #1 in the industry. |
| **End-to-end** | Merkle works *on* the business (strategy, transformation) and *in* the business (managed services, operations). Cover both when relevant. |

Merkle's persona is **coach, conductor, and choreographer** — an expert who makes the client the star. Copy should never make Merkle the hero. The client's outcome is the headline.


## 2. Voice — constant across all contexts

The voice does not change. Whether it's a case study or a slide headline or a tooltip:

**We sound:**
- Crisp, approachable, simple
- Smart over clever
- Caring and empathetic
- Passionate without iron (no hyperbole, no chest-beating)
- Oriented towards building, not theorising
- Outcome-led, not appearance-led

**We never sound:**
- Arrogant or sarcastic
- Full of self-importance
- Jargon-heavy or full of buzzwords
- Like a college essay (verbose, clause-heavy, deadline-padding)


## 3. Tone — shifts with audience and context

Voice stays constant. Tone shifts.

| Audience | Tone | Example framing |
|---|---|---|
| **Clients** | "We'll help you do it" | Empowering, collaborative, focused on their challenge |
| **Internal / our people** | "Let's go do this" | Energetic, rallying, peer-to-peer |
| **The market** | "We've done it before" | Authoritative, evidence-based, confident without arrogance |
| **Product users** | Clear, direct, low friction | Focus on the next step, not the feature |


## 4. Core writing mechanics

These rules apply across all three contexts unless a section below overrides them.

### Structure
- **Inverted pyramid always.** Most important thing first. The end is for restating it. Most readers won't reach the bottom.
- Short paragraphs: 2–3 sentences each.
- Topic sentence first, almost always. Don't make the reader hunt for the point.
- No more than 4–5 paragraphs without a subheading or visual break.

### Sentences
- Short and direct. Say what you mean.
- Avoid inverted structures, nested clauses, and excessive parenthetical phrases.
- Fragments are fine when they add rhythm or punch.
- Active voice. Passive voice is almost always worse. If you catch yourself writing a form of "to be," find a stronger verb.
- Avoid gerunds where possible ("we are delivering" → "we deliver").
- Minimise past participles. Prefer active, present-tense constructions.

### Words
- Prefer shorter words: *use* not *utilise*, *plan* not *strategise*, *change* not *transform* (unless transformation is the precise meaning), *drive* not *generate*, *increase* not *elevate*.
- If in doubt between two words, pick the shorter one.
- Strip adjectives and adverbs ruthlessly. Nouns and verbs carry the weight.
- **Banned "puffery" phrases** — these add no meaning and must be cut:
  - "truly remarkable"
  - "extraordinary transformation"
  - "mission-critical"
  - "unprecedented impact"
  - "game-changing"
  - "holistic approach"
  - "silver bullet"
  - "ecosystem" (unless it literally is one)
  - "right person, right place, right time"
  - "actionable" (say what the action is)
  - "innovative" / "innovation" (unless you can explain exactly how — and even then, show don't tell)
- **Watch word repetition.** If "experience," "transform," or "customer" appears in every sentence, rewrite.
- **No unnecessary exposition.** Everyone in this industry knows digital has changed, AI is important, and marketing is evolving. Skip the history lesson; get to the new news.

### Grammar and punctuation
- Oxford comma: always. "We use data, strategy, and technology."
- No ampersands (&) in running prose or headlines unless it's a proper name or initialism (R&D, AT&T). "Engagement and loyalty" — not "Engagement & Loyalty" in body copy.
- No single quotation marks for emphasis. Use italics instead.
- Em dashes for interruptive phrases — they give emphasis and interrupt cleanly.
- Semicolons connect two closely related independent clauses; they're your friend in complex lists.
- Capitalise only proper nouns. These are **not** proper nouns and should be lowercase: strategy, tactics, channels (e.g., social), departments or practice areas in general reference, audience, customer, consumer, brand, experience, digital, transformation, industry, sector.
- Don't capitalise for emphasis. Just don't.
- "Brand," "business," and "company" are not synonyms — use them with precision. Consider "organisation" or "team" when referring to the humans doing the work.
- "Customer" and "consumer" are not synonyms. "Customer" is more inclusive and covers B2B. Prefer it unless the B2C context is explicit.
- **Spell out numbers at the start of a sentence.** "Eighty-six percent of CMOs agree..." not "86% of CMOs agree..." Mid-sentence, numerals are fine.
- **Scare quotes signal intentional distance.** When Merkle uses quotes around a phrase like "innovation theater," it signals deliberate critique of an industry norm. Use this sparingly and only when the distancing is the point.

### The Golden Bale Rule
Applies to all copy: website, product, and slides.

**Lists of three or more: always "and," never "&," always Oxford comma.**

When items are listed together, write them out in full with a comma before the final "and" — regardless of whether the context is a headline, a label, a slide, or a nav item.

- Correct: *Promotions, Gamification, and Experiential Marketing*
- Incorrect: *Promotions, Gamification & Experiential Marketing*

The only exception is an established proper name or initialism (R&D, AT&T).

**Line breaks: the connector stays on the top line.**

When a two-part label or heading must break across two lines, keep "and" or "&" on the first line — not the second.

- Correct: *Loyalty Strategy &*  
  *Program Execution*
- Incorrect: *Loyalty Strategy*  
  *& Program Execution*

This applies to slide labels, product navigation, capability tiles, and any two-part heading that wraps. If "&" genuinely won't fit on the first line, rewrite the label so it does — or reconsider whether "&" is needed at all (see the ampersand rule above).

**If you're reviewing or producing layouts** — slides, web components, UI — flag any line-broken labels where the connector has drifted to the second line. This is a comms team responsibility as much as a copy one.

### Citations
- Always cite external stats and data. Use Chicago Manual of Style.
- Never invent or approximate a statistic. If you don't have the source, don't use the claim.
- Acceptable citation forms: footnote, in-text, URL link (last resort).
- Merkle's own research (CX Imperatives, B2B Superpowers, Loyalty Barometer, CMO Navigator) is citable as a primary source.


## 5. Context: Website copy

### What website copy does
It speaks to clients, prospects, and the market. Tone = "We'll help you do it" (client-facing) or "We've done it before" (market-facing).

### Page types and their patterns

**Homepage and brand pages**
- Lead with the brand promise or a crisp articulation of the problem Merkle solves.
- Follow with the "so what" — why it matters to the reader.
- Use concrete numbers as early as possible. "We've helped generate $35B+ in global annual revenue through Merkle-developed digital applications" — not "we help brands grow."
- Avoid generic opening lines. "In today's competitive landscape..." is dead copy. Cut it.

**Capability pages**
- Open with the client problem, not the Merkle capability.
- Use the "empathy is our superpower" principle: answer why the reader should care before describing what Merkle does.
- Good headline pattern: *[Client outcome] + [how Merkle enables it]* — "How Arhaus used identity recognition to make shoppers feel at home" is better than "Merkle provides 50% increase in B2B sales."
- Subheadings should move the story forward, not just label what follows.

**Case study pages**
- Title: client name + outcome-led headline. Not "Merkle helps [Client] with [service]." Yes: "Data-Driven Loyalty Fit for a King." No: "Merkle Provides CRM Solution for Burger King."
- Intro (Overview): 2–3 sentences. What did the client need? What did Merkle do? What changed?
- Challenge section: the client's problem in plain language. The reader should think "yes, that's real."
- Approach section: what Merkle actually did. Subheadings for each major workstream. Active voice throughout.
- Results: lead with numbers. "500% increase in loyalty database in under 8 months. 19.5% increase in purchase frequency." Numbers are more powerful than sentences. Use stats panels / callout numbers.
- Avoid making Merkle the subject of result sentences — let the outcome be the subject: "79% increase in digital revenue" not "Merkle delivered 79% increase in digital revenue."

**Numbers and stats on the web**
- Use exact figures over approximations wherever possible: "3,212" beats "thousands."
- Put numbers up front in callout cards, stat panels, and pull quotes.
- Always source.


## 6. Context: Digital product copy (microcopy and UX writing)

Product copy serves a different master than marketing copy. It is functional first, branded second.

### Principles
- **Clarity above all.** A confused user is a failed product.
- **One idea per piece of copy.** Labels, tooltips, and error messages should do one job.
- **Plain language.** If a non-specialist wouldn't understand it immediately, rewrite it.
- **Consistent terminology.** Pick a term ("customer," "user," "member") and use it everywhere in a product. Don't mix.
- **Action-oriented CTAs.** "Download report" beats "Report download." Verb first.

### Microcopy patterns

| Element | Do | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| **Button labels** | Verb + object: "Save report," "Start free trial," "Add to basket" | Vague: "Submit," "Click here," "Go" |
| **Empty states** | Explain why it's empty + what to do: "No results yet. Try adjusting your filters." | Just "No results" with nothing else |
| **Error messages** | Say what went wrong in plain language + what to do next. Apologise briefly if appropriate. | Technical jargon, blame the user, vague "Something went wrong" |
| **Tooltips** | One sentence. Answer "what is this?" or "why does it matter?" | Repeating the label; multi-sentence paragraphs |
| **Onboarding / in-app guidance** | Lead with benefit, then action: "See all your data in one place. Connect your first source to get started." | "Welcome to [product]! To begin, please navigate to..." |
| **Confirmation messages** | State what happened + what's next: "Report saved. It's available in your dashboard." | "Success!" with no context |

### Tone in product copy
Product copy should feel like a helpful colleague, not a marketing brochure. Keep the Merkle voice (smart, caring, direct) but dial back the market-facing confidence. In a product interface, the user is doing the work — copy supports them, not the brand.

### What to avoid in product copy
- Passive voice on any interactive element
- Capitalising every word in labels (Title Case for navigation; sentence case for everything else unless brand standard overrides)
- Marketing adjectives in functional copy ("powerful search," "seamless experience" as labels)
- Exclamation marks unless the context is genuinely celebratory and rare (first-time completion, major milestone)


## 7. Context: Presentations and slides

Slide copy is the context where the Brand Promise deck is the primary source of truth. Study it.

### How slides differ from web copy
- Copy is subordinate to the visual. It completes the visual, doesn't duplicate it.
- Slides are read in rooms, often quickly and from a distance. Every word must earn its place.
- A slide headline should be able to stand alone as the main point. If someone reads only the headers, they should still get the story.

### Headline writing for slides

Slide headlines are the single most important piece of copy on the slide. They should:
- State the point, not just label the topic.
- Be specific: "The next decade belongs to brands that win on experience" is a point. "Customer Experience" is a topic label.
- Use present or future tense to create forward momentum.
- Be short enough to read in under 3 seconds at presentation size.
- Occasionally use a question or provocation — but sparingly, and only if the slide answers it.

**Good headline patterns from Merkle brand:**
- Point of view: *"The stakes have never been higher"*
- Data-led: *"58% increase in brands considered for the average B2B purchase"*
- Declarative: *"We power the experience economy"*
- Challenge + resolution implicit: *"And we haven't seen anything yet"*

**Avoid:**
- Generic labels: "Our Approach," "Key Takeaways," "Overview" (as standalone headlines)
- Long headlines that wrap to three lines
- Headlines that end with a period and say nothing on their own

### Body copy on slides
- Use the minimum copy needed. If it can be a headline + a few bullets, that's better than two paragraphs.
- Keep bullets parallel: all noun phrases, or all verb phrases, not mixed.
- Bullets should be 1–2 sentences or tight phrases — not full paragraphs.
- Set up every bulleted list with an explanatory sentence that ends with a colon.
- Use a period after every bullet that is a full sentence or completes the stem. No period after a fragment.

### Stat panels and data callouts
Merkle uses large-number callouts extensively. When writing the supporting copy under a stat:
- The number is the headline. The copy explains it.
- Keep supporting copy to one line if possible, two at most.
- Don't start with "We" or "Merkle" — start with the outcome or the metric: "increase in digital revenue since 2022, Merkle helped..."
- Be precise: "79%" not "nearly 80%."

### Narrative arcs for decks
Merkle decks typically follow one of these structures:

1. **Market problem → Merkle position → Proof**: Start with the client's world (the experience economy, rising stakes), move to Merkle's response ("We power the experience economy"), back with data and case evidence.
2. **Challenge → Approach → Results**: Standard case study arc. Challenge section = the client's real pain. Approach = what Merkle actually did. Results = numbers first.
3. **Pillars + evidence**: For capability decks — state the three differentiators (Data-driven, Outcomes-obsessed, End-to-end), then support each with proof.

### Tone shifts for internal vs external decks
- **External / client decks**: "We'll help you do it." Empathetic, focused on their challenge. Make them the subject.
- **Internal / people decks**: "Let's go do this." More direct, rallying, collegial.
- **Market / conference decks**: "We've done it before." Authoritative, evidence-led.


## 8. Writing about AI — maintain this section as standards evolve

AI language at Merkle must be specific, grounded, and outcome-focused. The AI space moves fast; these are the current standards.

### The core Merkle AI position
From the AI Expertise page:

> "AI is fundamentally transforming how companies operate, how employees work, and how customers interact with brands. With deep roots in advanced data science and machine learning, we understand how to turn data-powered disruption into real business impact: focused strategy, organizational alignment, platforms that scale, and personalized experiences that drive loyalty."

Use **"real business impact"** and **"data-powered"** as anchors.

### The CX Imperatives AI position (2026 — most current)
The most authoritative framing from published Merkle research:

> *"Every company now has access to the same AI tools. Rushing to deploy technology isn't enough to differentiate. What matters is how companies deploy it: leading with outcomes instead of 'innovation theater,' solving real customer pain points instead of unintentionally creating new ones, and building trust at every stage of the journey."*

Key phrases to carry into any AI copy:
- **"innovation theater"** — when brands deploy AI for visibility rather than value. Always in scare quotes. Use sparingly; it signals deliberate industry critique.
- **"technology is the means to the end, not the end itself"** — the central AI principle for CX writing
- **"Consumers should feel your technology's benefits without noticing its mechanics"** — the design and experience standard for AI-powered products

### For AI in the enterprise context
- Lead with the problem: "Most generative AI pilots fail to deliver measurable value. Without clear priorities, defined success metrics, and aligned teams, intelligence never scales."
- Position Merkle as the partner who makes AI work at scale, not the inventor of AI.
- Reference the depth: "25+ years of expertise in applied data science, analytics, and machine learning."
- Be specific about what the AI does: "ML solutions to optimise truck routing for 3M+ monthly orders."

### For AI in customer experience
- Frame personalization as forward-looking, not backward-looking: *"Customers don't want a recap of their past actions. They want assistance with current needs or future decisions."*
- Use language around identity, intent, and real-time: "adaptive experiences that are identity-aware, conversational, and intent-driven."
- Always connect AI to trust. Security is consumers' primary concern — don't write about AI capability without acknowledging it. *"You need to secure their data, ensure your practices are sound, and reassure consumers that both are true."*
- Outcomes: "better experiences, stronger loyalty, and sustainable growth."

### For AI and design / creative (from Contradictory by Design)
- Frame human/AI as complementary: *"As AI takes on execution, it elevates the role of humans. Designers are moving from execution to direction."*
- The real creative risk of AI is sameness, not replacement: *"As AI floods the market with fast, competent work, sameness becomes a real threat. Human creativity and taste will matter more, because originality will be the only way to stand out."*
- For agentic AI and web/content: *"Today's websites must serve customers while also providing agents the information they need to surface brands during AI-based research."* Brands don't have to choose between designing well for humans and for generative AI — many of their needs are the same.

### AI language to avoid
- Avoid saying AI "transforms everything" without specifics — empty exposition rule applied to AI.
- Never claim Merkle "uses AI" without being able to say what type, for what purpose, with what result.
- Don't anthropomorphise AI tools ("our AI thinks," "it understands") unless technically defensible.
- Avoid buzzword soup: "agentic AI-powered intelligent automation solutions" — rewrite as what it actually does.
- "AI-powered" is acceptable as a modifier but must be followed by a specific noun and outcome: "AI-powered supply chain optimisation that saved $100M in lost revenue."
- Never write about AI capability without also addressing trust and security. Omitting it reads as tone-deaf.

- Sports analogies (never)

## 11. Quick reference by output type

### Writing a case study page
1. Client name + outcome headline (not capability label)
2. 2–3 sentence overview: problem, Merkle's role, result
3. Challenge section: client's real pain, 2–3 short paragraphs
4. Approach section: subheadings per workstream, active voice
5. Results: numbers as callouts, one-line support copy per stat
6. Source all stats

### Writing a slide headline
1. State the point, not the topic
2. Under 10 words preferred
3. Present or future tense
4. Test: can it stand alone and be understood?

### Writing a CTA
1. Verb + object: "Explore case studies," "Download the report," "Talk to us"
2. Never: "Click here," "Learn more" (without context), "Submit"
3. Match the CTA to the page's commitment level — "Contact us" is a big ask; "Read the story" is not

### Writing microcopy
1. Sentence case unless navigation standard overrides
2. One job per piece of copy
3. Tell the user what to do, not how the system works
4. Error messages: what went wrong + what to do next

### Writing about Merkle for the market
1. Lead with the client's world, not Merkle's capabilities
2. Back every claim with a number or a named example
3. Merkle is the coach — the client is the star
4. End on what's possible, not on what Merkle is
